# Related Work and Scope (draft)

Compute Express Link (CXL) is an open, cache-coherent interconnect that extends PCIe with memory semantics and supports capacity expansion, tiering, and multi-host memory pooling across CXL 1.0--3.0~\cite{cxl_intro}. A CXL switch is therefore more than a passive fan-out: when several hosts or sources have eligible requests for a shared downstream device, the switch must decide which request receives the next service opportunity. Pond shows that pooling across 8--16 sockets can reduce DRAM cost by 7\% while remaining within 1--5\% of same-NUMA-node allocations across 158 workloads~\cite{pond}. TPP manages page placement between local DRAM and a slower CXL tier~\cite{tpp}, and DirectCXL demonstrates directly accessible CXL.mem pooling with roughly a $7\times$ advantage over RDMA-based pooling in its tested workloads~\cite{directcxl}. These systems establish the value of shared memory, but their primary evaluation targets allocation, placement, or end-to-end application behavior. They do not isolate the cost of the switch-side arbitration that resolves simultaneous eligibility. Levis et al.\ question whether the benefits of pooling justify its cost and complexity~\cite{case_pool}, and recent work examines CXL pools as an alternative to costly PCIe switching~\cite{my_cxl_pool}. Those arguments make arbitration a first-order design question rather than an assumed-free detail.

Memory scheduling has long treated fairness as an objective distinct from throughput. Stall-time fair memory scheduling equalizes the DRAM-related slowdown experienced by each thread, rather than equalizing bandwidth, and reports a reduction in the maximum-to-minimum slowdown ratio from $5.26\times$ to $1.4\times$ while improving weighted speedup by 7.6\% on its 8-core workloads~\cite{stfm}. Fair queuing applies network-style quality-of-service (QoS) to shared memory and reduces variance in achieved bandwidth~\cite{fqmem}. ATLAS periodically orders threads by attained service to improve throughput across multiple memory controllers~\cite{atlas}, while TCM separates latency-sensitive and bandwidth-sensitive threads and shuffles priority within the bandwidth-sensitive cluster~\cite{tcm}. These policies require per-thread history, ranking, periodic sorting, or priority state. Their published evaluations quantify system performance and fairness, not the standard-cell cost of that state. BLISS makes the hardware consequence explicit: a two-group blacklisting design reduces critical-path latency and area by 79\% and 43\%, respectively, relative to TCM in a 32\,nm RTL study, while improving performance and fairness over prior schedulers~\cite{bliss}. The lesson is not that one policy is universally best, but that fairness mechanisms consume state and logic; a credible comparison must therefore include the metadata each policy requires.

FAI-CXL is the closest prior work because it integrates cacheline-granularity weighted interleaving and adaptive fairness-aware scheduling into a CXL switch~\cite{fai}. Its evaluation uses ChampSim and Ramulator and reports application-level gains of up to 21\% IPC and 17\% throughput; its hardware-cost section estimates that, under a 4\,TB pool, 1\,GB block size, and 16-host assumption, the added storage is below 200\,KB and the lookup and priority arbitration add less than 2\,ns to the data path~\cite{fai}. Those estimates are useful first-order bounds, but they are not a synthesized comparison of alternative RTL schedulers. Other CXL studies characterize interference on real systems~\cite{cxl_interplay}, contention-aware caching for pooled memory~\cite{contention_cache}, and shared-pool interference in HPC workloads~\cite{hpc_pool}. CXL-DMSim provides a full-system simulator validated against FPGA- and ASIC-based devices with an average error of 3.4\%~\cite{cxldmsim}, and memory-side directory work shows that fabric metadata and coherence policy can themselves affect performance~\cite{redit}. Synchronization studies further show that software-visible coordination remains part of the CXL memory story~\cite{sync_cxl}, while adaptive coherence proposals compare hardware- and software-managed sharing~\cite{rethinking_pool}. Collectively, these studies establish contention, interleaving, coherence, and synchronization as real concerns, but they leave the controlled hardware cost of alternative switch arbitration policies unmeasured.

Hardware scheduling also has a substantial implementation literature. Memory access scheduling reorders requests to exploit DRAM bank and row locality; conservative and aggressive reordering improved bandwidth by 40\% and 93\%, respectively, on the media traces studied by Rixner et al.~\cite{rixner}. Crossbar schedulers for input-queued switches use multiple round-robin arbiters, and the Tiny Tera design configured its crossbar once every 51\,ns~\cite{gupta}. Weighted fair queuing requires tag computation and sorting; a 130\,nm packet-sorting circuit supported 40\,Gb/s line rates~\cite{wfq_sort}. These works show that arbitration and fair queuing are not free, but their switch and network contexts differ from CXL memory semantics. BLISS remains the closest hardware-cost precedent for memory scheduling: it reports area in $\mu$m$^2$ and critical path in nanoseconds from synthesized RTL, but its DRAM-controller boundary, workload set, and commercial 32\,nm technology differ from ours, so its numbers are not a numerical baseline~\cite{bliss}. A NoC switch-arbiter study likewise reports area and power in a 180\,nm process~\cite{arbiter_ppa}, reinforcing that absolute costs are technology- and boundary-dependent. Our contribution is therefore not the first hardware study of scheduling, but a like-for-like comparison within one CXL-oriented descriptor datapath and SAED32 synthesis flow.

Across these lines of work, system studies show that CXL pooling creates shared-resource contention; CXL-specific studies characterize interference and propose interleaving or fairness policies; and hardware studies show that scheduling logic has nonzero area and timing cost. What remains missing is a controlled, like-for-like PPA comparison of alternative arbitration policies under one datapath, FIFO organization, descriptor width, and synthesis boundary for a CXL-oriented switch slice. This report addresses that narrow gap. We implement FCFS, round robin, and an FAI-inspired policy in one SystemVerilog framework, keep the descriptor and FIFO datapath common, and report complete-switch, policy-subtree, and FIFO-storage costs separately, with RTL/netlist equivalence as a correctness guard~\cite{artifact}. We do not claim a new scheduling algorithm, a CXL-compliant switch, application-level fairness, maximum frequency, or physical signoff. FAI-CXL's Algorithm~2 also contains a specification ambiguity: its prose describes retaining the previous priority when a source enters the buffer, while its pseudocode assigns the target class~\cite{fai}. We treat Main's hysteresis and an immediate-target variant as two source-grounded interpretations, not a cycle-identical reproduction, and quantify their behavioral and area differences. The result is an implementation-grounded PPA case study whose claims stop at the measured pre-layout boundary.
